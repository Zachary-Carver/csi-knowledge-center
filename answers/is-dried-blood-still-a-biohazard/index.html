Is Dried Blood Still a Biohazard?

Yes. Dried blood should still be treated as potentially hazardous until the affected area has been properly assessed and remediated. Drying does not prove that a surface or material is safe.

The visible stain is also not always the full affected area. Blood can migrate through carpet, padding, flooring seams, subflooring, baseboards, upholstery, furniture, drywall and vehicle materials. When contamination reaches porous or concealed materials, surface cleaning alone may not address the entire problem.

Does dried blood become safe once it is no longer wet?

No. Appearance and moisture level do not establish that a contaminated material is safe. Blood-contaminated surfaces and materials should be handled using appropriate precautions until they have been properly evaluated and remediated.

Can dried blood remain below the visible surface?

Yes. Depending on the amount of fluid and the materials involved, contamination may be present in padding, seams, underlayment, subflooring or other porous and concealed areas even when the visible surface appears dry.

Why is professional assessment different from ordinary cleaning?

Ordinary cleaning focuses on visible soil and appearance. Biohazard remediation focuses on identifying affected materials, controlling contamination and determining what can be cleaned and what may require removal.

Important limitations

This article provides general educational information. Scene conditions vary. For immediate danger, an active crime or a medical emergency, call 911 and do not disturb the scene.
